## Codex draft script (Claude to review)

**Set-up and number rules.** Brian shows a small car bolted to a steel arm, with the arm turning around a marked centre. A cord hangs from the car. A sideways arrow, the cord's lean and a meter all grow with the same reading. Brian defines speed as distance travelled during a set time and says kilometres per hour (“km/h”) is the dial's unit. He defines metres (“m”) as the arm's unit and explains that the meter uses g for Earth's gravitational acceleration near the ground. The starting dial says 64.4 km/h and the arm says 50 m. Later, Brian defines metres per second (“m/s”) as metres travelled each second. One kilometre is 1,000 m and one hour is 3,600 s, so km/h divided by 3.6 gives m/s.

Brian defines velocity as speed in a direction. Acceleration means how quickly velocity changes. Metres per second squared (“m/s²”) means a speed change of that many m/s during each second. One g means Earth's gravitational acceleration near the ground, about 9.81 m/s². A turning car keeps changing direction, so it needs a sideways push to keep changing its velocity. The driver feels that push. Speed matters twice: a faster car has more velocity to change, and it changes direction sooner around the same turn.

After defining those terms, the panel gives the game's rule: `G-force (g) = (speed in m/s)² ÷ radius in m ÷ 9.81 m/s²`. Dividing by 9.81 m/s² compares the sideways acceleration with one g, so the result counts how many g the driver feels. Worked sums show the precise km/h value for the selected dial setting, convert it by 3.6 to m/s, and show five decimal places so the result calculates. The dial rounds that setting to one decimal place. The interactive result and every pass condition still use the existing `lateralG(speed, radius)` function also used by the challenge. While a guess is open, the panel shows only the general formula and definitions, with no worked result or live reading that gives away the answer. Panel text says “this test” or names the turning-arm model; it never says “bench”.
